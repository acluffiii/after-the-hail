How shops get listed in this directory

Our directory is empty, and that’s on purpose. Texas has no single state list of licensed dent shops to copy, a maps pin doesn’t prove anyone will be there in six months, and online ratings can be bought or faked. This page explains what public evidence we need to see before a business name goes up, what isn’t enough, and what we won’t do. The legal points are cited to Texas statutes, state agencies, and the FTC, all linked below.

Read this first. This page describes our own editorial rule. It isn’t legal advice, and it doesn’t name, rate, or recommend any shop. A listing here, when there are any, will mean we could verify who a business is and where it can be found. It won’t mean we vouch for the quality of its work. The one Reddit post quoted below is one person’s experience, not data.

The short version

  1. No shop is listed yet. The directory will grow one business at a time, and only after we’ve opened public sources ourselves.
  2. Every listing needs a findable business and a public record behind it: its own website or a public maps listing, plus a matching Texas entity or assumed-name record.
  3. A recommendation alone isn’t enough, whether it comes from Reddit, X, a flyer, or a tent sign. Neither is a phone number by itself.
  4. We don’t publish star ratings, review counts, or “best of” rankings, and we don’t sell listings or placement.
  5. This site is built by DentDOG PDR, the team behind a PDR estimator app. Whether a business uses that app has nothing to do with whether it’s listed.

Why there’s no state list to copy

The Texas DMV’s Smart Repairs guide says it plainly: “In Texas, the state or local government does not license repair shops. There is not a test a mechanic must take before he opens a shop to perform motor vehicle repairs.” The Texas Department of Insurance says it “doesn’t regulate service providers, like body shops and tow truck companies,” and sends body-shop questions and complaints to the Texas Attorney General (800-621-0508).

That leaves owners checking the business themselves. Here’s one example of what can go wrong when there’s nothing to check. In a July 2025 r/plano post, an owner described two men in a pickup who knocked on the door offering mobile dent repair. The owner said the side of the car was “completely covered over in wax so the repair area was not visible” when the men asked for payment, and that wiping the wax off showed a repaint that “didn’t even match.” The owner refused to pay until they could see the work, and said the men got “borderline hostile.” A business like that couldn’t meet the rule below, because there would be nothing public to verify.

Sources: TxDMV, Smart Repairs (PDF), p. 1; TDI, “Not sure who to call?”, Auto section; r/plano, “Beware of dent repair scammers” (July 2025; one owner’s account).

What we check before a name appears

1. A real business you can find later

We start with the business’s own website or a public maps listing. We record the business name, city, the source URL, and the date we opened it. Google’s own rules for Business Profiles show why we treat a maps listing as a starting point and not as proof:

  • A profile should be for “your actual, real-world location.” A business that rents a mailing address it doesn’t operate from (a “virtual office”) “isn’t eligible.”
  • Businesses that show an address “should maintain permanent fixed signage of their business name at the address.”
  • A business that “travels to customers” can have a service-area profile, and Google says service-area businesses should hide their address from customers.

Those are Google’s requirements for businesses. They don’t show that any particular listing follows them. A mobile PDR tech with a service-area profile can still be listed here if the other checks match. In that case we’ll say “mobile, no storefront” so you know there may not be a shop to drive back to.

Source: Google Business Profile Help, “Guidelines for representing your business on Google” (Address and Service-area businesses sections).

2. A name that matches a Texas record

The name on the website or sign should match a public record. Texas has two main ways to check:

  • Corporations and LLCs. The Comptroller’s free Franchise Tax Account Status Search lets you search by entity name, taxpayer number or EIN, or Secretary of State file number. The Secretary of State’s SOSPortal also has business records, but the SOS says there is “a $1.00 statutorily authorized fee associated with each search.”
  • Trade names (“doing business as”). Under Texas Business & Commerce Code Chapter 71, a corporation or LLC that regularly does business under an assumed name files a certificate with the Secretary of State (§71.103). A sole proprietor or partnership using an assumed name files with the county clerk in each county where it has premises, or where it does business if it has no premises (§71.054). The Secretary of State and every county clerk keep an alphabetical index of assumed names (§71.154).

A missing certificate isn’t automatically a red flag. For an individual, Chapter 71 only counts a name as “assumed” if it doesn’t include the owner’s surname (§71.002), so a tech who works under their own name doesn’t have to file. We need the name a customer sees to lead to some record we can open: an entity, an assumed-name filing, or a person’s own name on a business website.

Sources: Texas Comptroller, Franchise Tax Account Status Search; Texas Secretary of State, SOSDirect / SOSPortal; Texas Business & Commerce Code Chapter 71, Assumed Business or Professional Name.

3. A body-shop registration number, if the shop shows one

Texas does have a registration law for body repair, even though there’s no licensing test. Occupations Code Chapter 2304 defines a “repair facility” as “a person that engages in the business of repairing or replacing the nonmechanical exterior or interior body parts of a damaged motor vehicle,” and says a repair facility “shall register” with the Texas Commission on Environmental Quality. Under that chapter:

  • It doesn’t apply to a repair facility in a county with a population of 50,000 or less (§2304.002).
  • Registration is a sworn application with a $50 fee, and it expires after one year (§2304.053).
  • The certificate must be displayed “in a location readily visible to a customer paying for repairs” (§2304.101).
  • The registration number has to appear “on each repair estimate, repair order, or correspondence” and “in each advertisement for motor vehicle repairs” (§2304.102).
  • TCEQ is required to publish a yearly list of registered names and addresses, plus registrations that were revoked, suspended, or surrendered (§2304.058).

Here’s how we use that. If a shop’s estimates or ads show a Chapter 2304 registration number, we’ll record it with the listing. We haven’t yet found TCEQ’s published list or an online lookup, and we haven’t seen TCEQ guidance on whether a mobile PDR tech counts as a “repair facility.” Until we can check a number against TCEQ’s own records, we won’t treat a missing number as a reason to exclude a business, and we won’t call a business “registered” just because it says so. Keep in mind that this is a registration, not a skills test. TxDMV’s point that there’s no licensing exam still stands.

Source: Texas Occupations Code Chapter 2304, Nonmechanical Repairs to Motor Vehicles.

What doesn’t get a business listed

  • A Reddit or X recommendation by itself. We use those threads to find the questions owners ask, not to build a phone book.
  • A tent, a flyer under a wiper, or a knock on the door, with no website, address, or public record behind it.
  • A phone number or social media page with no business name we can match to a record.
  • Payment. Listings and placement aren’t for sale. If we ever add affiliate links elsewhere on the site, they’ll be disclosed and won’t buy a listing (see About).

Why we don’t copy star ratings

We won’t republish star ratings or review counts, and we won’t write “top-rated” or “best of” about any shop. Part of the reason is that we can’t check them. The FTC’s Rule on the Use of Consumer Reviews and Testimonials (16 CFR Part 465, adopted in August 2024) shows the kinds of problems it targets:

  • Fake reviews that misrepresent whether the reviewer exists or used the service (§465.2).
  • Paying or rewarding people for reviews “expressing a particular sentiment, whether positive or negative” (§465.4).
  • Reviews by a business’s own officers or managers without a clear disclosure of that relationship (§465.5).

The rule doesn’t say that any particular hail shop has broken it. It just shows why a rating copied from somewhere else isn’t something we can verify. If you want to read reviews, read them on the platform where they were posted, and read the bad ones as well as the good ones.

Source: 16 CFR Part 465, Rule on the Use of Consumer Reviews and Testimonials (eCFR).

Our own connection, stated plainly

After the Hail is built by DentDOG PDR, the team behind a PDR estimator app (see About). The same FTC rule makes it a violation for a business to “materially misrepresent” that a website it controls “provides independent reviews or opinions” about a category that includes its own products or services (§465.6). We don’t review or rank shops, and here is how we handle the connection:

  • Whether a shop uses, or doesn’t use, the DentDOG app has no effect on whether it’s listed or where it appears.
  • If a business connected to DentDOG PDR is ever listed, the listing will say so.
  • The same verification rule applies to every business.

If you run a PDR shop or work as a tech

We aren’t taking submissions yet. When we do, this page will say how. There will be no fee. When we start adding listings, these are the things we’ll look for:

  • The same business name on your website or maps listing, your estimates, and a Texas record (an entity, an assumed-name certificate, or your own name).
  • A permanent address, or an honest service-area listing if you’re mobile.
  • Your Chapter 2304 registration number on estimates and ads, if you’re a repair facility in a county over 50,000 people. That’s what the statute requires, and it gives customers something they can check.
  • A way for a customer to reach you after the storm crews leave town. That’s the question owners ask most in the threads we read.

How a listing comes down

We’ll remove or flag a listing if its source goes dead, the business name stops matching a public record, or the business says it has closed. We’ll keep the date we last checked each source next to the listing.
