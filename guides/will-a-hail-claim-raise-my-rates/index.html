Will a hail claim raise my car insurance rates in Texas?

It’s the question that stops a lot of owners from filing. Online you’ll hear that hail is an “act of God” and can’t count against you, and you’ll also hear that any claim can raise your premium. We read what Texas law and the Texas Department of Insurance (TDI) actually say. The real answer is in between, and the details can help you decide whether to file, pay cash, or live with the dents.

Read this first. This is not legal or insurance advice. We quote the Texas rules and TDI pages we opened and point out where they stop. No one outside your insurance company can tell you how much your premium will change. That depends on your insurer’s filed rating plan, and this page doesn’t invent an “average increase.” Posts quoted below are one person’s story each, not data.

The short version

  1. A paid hail claim can affect your auto premium. TDI: “your home and auto premiums can go up if you file claims. You could also lose any discounts you’re getting for being claim free.” The rule that bars higher premiums for weather claims applies to home policies. TDI doesn’t list it for auto.
  2. Your insurer can’t drop you at renewal solely because of a hail claim. Texas rule 28 TAC §5.7016 bars a refusal to renew a personal auto policy based solely on “claims involving damage from a weather-related incident that do not involve a collision, such as, but not limited to, hail.” That protects your renewal. It doesn’t freeze your price.
  3. A claim that isn’t paid, or a question you ask, can’t be the sole reason for a rate change. Insurance Code §1953.051(b) bars that for personal auto policies. But read the fine print below on what counts as “just a question.”
  4. Your claim will probably show up for other insurers too. Most companies check the CLUE claims database, which keeps up to seven years of auto claims. You can get your own report free once a year.
  5. Compare repair cost to your deductible first. TDI: “If the cost of repairs is about the same or less than your deductible, you may decide it’s not worth filing a claim.”

The location isn’t stated, it isn’t a rate statistic, and we don’t know what else changed on that policy.

Both are real experiences. The rules below explain why both can happen.

1. Premiums: TDI says yes, a claim can count

TDI’s tip “Will my premium go up if I file a claim?” starts: “It depends on the type of claim and how many you file. But, yes, your home and auto premiums can go up if you file claims. You could also lose any discounts you’re getting for being claim free.”

Then it splits the protections by type of policy:

  • Home and auto companies “can’t charge you more for” claims “that the company didn’t pay,” including denied claims, or for “calling your company or agent to ask questions about your policy or the claims filing process.”
  • Only home companies “can’t charge you more for” “claims for damage from natural causes, including weather.”

That split explains the “act of God” rumor. Texas does have a weather-claim protection on premiums, but it’s in the homeowners rules. Insurance Code §551.107, which sets the renewal and surcharge rules that exclude “a loss caused by natural causes,” says it “applies only to a standard fire, homeowners, or farm or ranch owners insurance policy.” For a car, TDI’s auto guide lists “your driving record and claims history” first among the things companies weigh when setting a premium.

Sources: TDI, “Will my premium go up if I file a claim?” (last updated 26 February 2026); Texas Insurance Code Chapter 551, §551.107(a); TDI auto insurance guide, “Understanding premiums.”

How much, if anything, a claim changes your premium depends on your insurer’s rating plan. TDI’s guide says: “We don’t approve rates in advance, but if we find that an insurance company’s rates are too high, we can require it to pay refunds.” TDI’s personal-auto filing checklist says each insurer files its rates and “supplementary rating information” with the commissioner. So the honest answer to “how much?” is to ask your insurer or agent, in writing, how a paid comprehensive claim is treated under your policy’s rating plan and claim-free discount.

Sources: TDI auto insurance guide, “Understanding premiums”; TDI, Review Requirements Checklist – Personal Automobile, “File & Use.”

2. Renewal: hail alone can’t get you dropped

This is the protection people usually mean when they say a hail claim “can’t count.” It’s a rule about renewal, not about price. Under 28 Texas Administrative Code §5.7016(a), none of the following “may be used by any insurer as the sole basis for a refusal to renew a personal auto policy”:

  • “claims involving damage from a weather-related incident that do not involve a collision, such as, but not limited to, hail, flood, tornado, winds, or hurricanes”;
  • claims for damage “by contact with animals or fowls”;
  • damage “caused by flying gravel, missiles, or falling objects” (but after three of those losses in 36 months, the insurer may raise the deductible at renewal); and
  • towing and labor (after four of those claims in 36 months, the insurer may drop that coverage).

Other claims where you “cannot reasonably be considered to be at fault” are protected too, “unless there are two or more such claims or accidents in any 12-month period” (§5.7016(b)). The 2026 Consumer Bill of Rights that insurers must give auto policyholders repeats this as item 24, “Not-at-fault claims,” and gives hail, wind, and flood as examples.

Watch these limits:

  • “Sole basis.” The rule bars a nonrenewal based solely on the hail claim. It doesn’t stop a company from acting on other lawful grounds.
  • “Do not involve a collision.” Hail falling on a parked car is the clear case. If you drive into a hailstorm and hit something, ask how the claim is coded.
  • Same company only. §5.7016(d) defines refusal to renew as refusing to renew “in the same insurance company which originally issued the policy.” A new insurer can still see the claim on your CLUE report (section 4).
  • Notice. Under the Bill of Rights, a company must tell you “at least 60 days before your policy expires” that it isn’t renewing, “or you can require them to renew your policy,” and must “fully explain in writing the reasons.”

Sources: 28 Tex. Admin. Code §5.7016 (via Cornell LII); TDI, Consumer Bill of Rights – Personal Automobile Insurance, 2026 version (PDF), items 21, 22, 24, and 31; TDI personal-auto review checklist, “Nonrenewal”; TDI auto insurance guide, “Losing your insurance.”

3. Unpaid claims, and the “I was only asking” trap

Texas Insurance Code §1953.051(b) says a personal auto rating plan may not “assign a rate consequence solely to” (A) “a consumer inquiry … made by an applicant or insured” or (B) “a claim filed by an insured under a personal automobile insurance policy that is not paid or payable under the policy.” The Bill of Rights puts it simply: “Your insurance company can’t change your premium solely because of a claim you file that is not paid or payable under your policy.”

That matters for hail in two ways:

  • A claim that ends at or under the deductible. If the adjuster’s estimate comes in below your deductible, nothing is payable. Under the statute, that claim alone can’t carry a rate consequence. Still, ask how the insurer is recording it.
  • Questions vs. claims. The Bill of Rights (item 29) says you can ask “questions about whether the policy will cover a loss, unless the question is about damage (a) that occurred, and (b) that results in an investigation or claim.” TDI’s CLUE tip adds: “make sure you’re clear about whether you’re filing a claim or just asking a question about your policy.” If you call about damage that already happened and the company opens a claim, you may be past the “just a question” protection.

Sources: Texas Insurance Code Chapter 1953, §1953.051; TDI Consumer Bill of Rights – Personal Auto (2026), items 20 and 29; TDI, “How to get a CLUE about your claims history.”

4. CLUE: where your claim history lives

TDI’s auto guide: “Most companies use the Comprehensive Loss Underwriting Exchange (CLUE) to learn your claims history. A company can charge you more or refuse to sell you a policy based on the information in your CLUE report.” TDI’s premium tip adds that because most companies use CLUE, “they can learn about home or auto claims you’ve filed, even if the claim was with another insurance company.”

  • What’s on it. TDI says insurers report the “date of loss,” “loss type,” and “amount paid on the claim,” along with the policy number, claim number, and insurer. Reports cover the past seven years and list claims on a vehicle “even if you weren’t the owner at the time.”
  • Getting yours. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau says LexisNexis C.L.U.E. “will provide one free report every 12 months if you request it,” and that requesting your own report doesn’t hurt your credit scores. The CFPB lists consumer.risk.lexisnexis.com and 866-897-8126. TDI’s tip and auto guide list 866-312-8076. Use whichever one connects.
  • Fixing errors. TDI says you can contact LexisNexis “to dispute wrong information or to add an explanation.” The CFPB notes that under the FCRA, companies must investigate a dispute free of charge.

Sources: TDI auto insurance guide; TDI premium tip; TDI CLUE tip (last updated 14 October 2024); CFPB, LexisNexis C.L.U.E. & Telematics OnDemand.

5. File, pay cash, or live with it?

No page can make this decision for you, but these are the steps the official sources support, in order:

  1. Find your comprehensive deductible. It’s on your declarations page. TDI: “The first page of your policy is the declaration page. It has a summary of your policy, including your coverages, dollar limits, and deductibles.” Hail falls under comprehensive, not collision.
  2. Get a written repair estimate before you file. TDI suggests getting estimates and comparing them to the deductible. Our cited cost estimator can give you a rough range first. It isn’t a quote, and hail damage is often more than it looks in a driveway.
  3. If the repair is near or below the deductible, TDI says “you may decide it’s not worth filing a claim.” Paying cash or leaving the dents avoids a paid claim on your record. You absorb the cost or the cosmetic damage instead.
  4. If the repair is well above the deductible, weigh the payout against possibly losing a claim-free discount or paying more at renewal. Ask your insurer or agent how a paid comprehensive claim affects your rate. Under §1953.051(b), a general question about your policy can’t be the sole basis for a rate consequence. A question about damage that has already happened can turn into a claim, so be clear which one you’re doing.
  5. If you owe money on the car, TDI says your lender requires comprehensive and collision. Read your loan or lease terms before deciding to leave damage unrepaired.
  6. If you get a nonrenewal notice after a hail-only claim, ask for the written reasons (Bill of Rights item 31). If you think the nonrenewal was improper, TDI says “you may complain to us.”

Sources: TDI auto insurance guide (deductibles and dollar limits; “Losing your insurance”); TDI premium tip, “Consider your deductible”; TDI Consumer Bill of Rights – Personal Auto (2026).

Common claims, checked

  • “Hail is an act of God, so it can’t raise my rates.” Not under TDI’s guidance for auto. The no-higher-premium rule for weather claims is listed for home policies. For auto, the weather protection covers nonrenewal (28 TAC §5.7016).
  • “Any claim gets you dropped.” A non-collision hail claim can’t be the sole basis for a refusal to renew a personal auto policy in Texas.
  • “Even calling them raises your rate.” A consumer inquiry alone can’t carry a rate consequence (§1953.051(b)). Reporting damage that leads to a claim is different.
  • “A denied claim still counts against you.” Not for pricing on its own: a claim “not paid or payable” can’t be the sole basis for a rate consequence.
